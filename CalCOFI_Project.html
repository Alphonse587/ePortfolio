Case study: an oxygen model that holds up in future years

A random forest trained on 1949–2009 data predicts oxygen in 2010–2021 samples with R² = 0.98, cutting the error of a linear model by more than half.

The question

It changed after exploring the data.

Using depth, temperature, salinity, location and month, how accurately can we predict dissolved oxygen in years the model has never seen?

Low-oxygen water (hypoxia) stresses or kills fish and invertebrates. Oxygen is slower and more expensive to measure than temperature and salinity, which modern sensors record continuously. A reliable model could estimate oxygen wherever only temperature and salinity readings exist.

I started from a common question for this dataset, whether salinity predicts temperature. Salinity alone explained only 24% of the variation (R² = 0.24), and both are read by the same sensor anyway. Oxygen had strong but curved relationships with the other variables and is the hard one to measure, so I switched to predicting it. A shift in oxygen levels over time added a second test: does a model trained on the past keep working?

Data cleaning: 895,371 rows down to 403,814

The CalCOFI Bottle Database comes from the California Cooperative Oceanic Fisheries Investigations, which has sampled a fixed grid of stations off California several times a year since 1949. It arrives as two tables, casts and bottles, which I joined on the cast ID. I kept 45% of the original rows, and each cut has a reason.

  • Interpolated rows removed. 37% of rows were values interpolated from neighbouring bottles, not measurements. Keeping them would inflate accuracy.
  • No imputation of the target. Filling in missing oxygen would mean grading the model on made-up answers. Nutrients and chlorophyll were 50–73% missing, so I dropped those columns.
  • Physical limits instead of a blind outlier rule. The raw data held 99 °C water and −200 mL/L oxygen. A 1.5×IQR rule would also delete real warm surface and cold deep water, so I kept −2 to 35 °C, salinity 30–37.5 and oxygen 0–12 mL/L.

What the data showed: oxygen bends with depth

The relationships are strong but curved, which pointed me toward a non-linear model.

Salinity and temperature carry the signal

Oxygen correlates with salinity (r = −0.82) and temperature (r = 0.79). Location and year correlate weakly (|r| ≤ 0.14).

No steady decline since 1949

The full-record trend is flat (+0.05 mL/L per decade), but levels since 2000 are lower than in the 1980s–90s.

Sampling changed over time

The 1970s have only four qualifying years, and the method moved from bottles to CTD sensors in 1993, so part of the pattern may come from how samples were taken.

Models: split by year, not at random

Samples from the same cast are metres apart and nearly identical. A random split puts near-copies in both training and test sets. I trained on 1949–2009 (348,838 rows) and tested on 2010–2021 (54,976 rows), which matches the real use: predicting future measurements.

I left out features that would leak the answer (oxygen saturation is calculated from oxygen), density (calculated from temperature and salinity) and year, so the model predicts from the water's physics rather than memorising the trend.

Random forest: 80 trees, max depth 20, min 5 samples per leaf, trained on a 200,000-row subsample.

Results: the relationships held across 60 years

On a random 80/20 split the same forest scored R² = 0.981, only slightly above the year-based 0.978. The model isn't just memorising nearby samples: what it learned from 1949–2009 still holds in 2010–2021.

Example: a summer sample from 250 m in the Southern California Bight at 8.5 °C and salinity 34.15 is predicted to hold 1.76 mL/L of oxygen, low enough to stress many fish.

  • Salinity and temperature carry almost all the signal.
  • The relationships are non-linear, which is why the forest beats linear regression.
  • Mid-depth oxygen has been lower since 2000 than in the 1980s–90s.

Limitations

  • Bottle-to-CTD change in 1993 may add measurement differences.
  • The 1970s have few oxygen measurements.
  • Applies only to the California Current's water masses.
  • A random forest can't predict beyond values seen in training, so it may miss future extremes.

Next steps

  • Add nitrate and phosphate for the post-1980 period.
  • Try gradient boosting, tuned with cross-validation grouped by cruise.
  • Add El Niño (ENSO) indices to explain year-to-year swings.
  • Use the model to fill the 1970s gap and study the long-term trend.

Reflection

The biggest lesson was that cleaning decisions shape the result as much as the model does. Dropping interpolated rows and splitting by year both made my scores harder to earn, but they made the scores honest. I also learned to let exploration change the question: the first idea was easy to answer and not very useful.

These habits carry into healthcare data, where records are also repeated, imputed or flagged, and where a model has to work on next year's patients, not last year's.
